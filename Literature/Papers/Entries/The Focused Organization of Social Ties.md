## Abstract

Sociologists since Simmel have been interested in social circles as essential features of friendship networks. Although network analysis has been increasingly used to uncover patterns among social relationships, theoretical explanations of these patterns have been inadequate. This paper presents a theory of the social organization of friendship ties. The approach is based upon Homans’s concepts of activities, interactions, and sentiments and upon the concept of extra-network foci organizing social activities and interaction. The theory is contrasted with Heider’s balance theory. Implications for transitivity, network bridges, and density of personal networks are discussed and presented as propositions. The focus theory is shown to help explain patterns of friendships in the 1965–66 Detroit Area Study. This paper is intended as a step toward the development of integrated theory to explain interrelationships between networks and other aspects of social structure. Implications for data analysis are discussed.

## Model
> Model extracted via LLM according to [[Model Notation]]

This is a theoretical account, not an executable simulation. Its core constructs and propositions are:[^r2][^r3]

- **Focus:** extra-network context organizing shared activities and interaction; its size, constraint, and compatibility shape opportunities for ties.
- **Tie formation:** shared activities increase interaction and sentiment, making friendship ties more likely.
- **Transitivity:** more likely when two ties share a focus, the focus is more constraining, more shared foci overlap, distinct foci are more compatible, or the two existing ties take more of the intermediary’s time, energy, and emotion.
- **Bridging:** focus-based ties are less likely to bridge than ties not based on foci. Among focus-based ties, bridging is more likely when foci are smaller and less constraining, when fewer foci are involved, when distinct foci are less compatible, and when less time, energy, and emotion are invested.
- **Personal-network density:** predicted to increase when associates come from fewer, more constraining, or more compatible foci, when ego shares more foci with each associate, and when ties take more of ego’s time, effort, and emotion. The Detroit illustration reports fully connected friend triads for 57% of single-source versus 17% of three-source networks; it supplies suggestive evidence rather than critical tests of all propositions.[^r4]

### What does it represent?
A theory linking social contexts outside the network to friendship formation and resulting network structure, building on Homans’s activities, interactions, and sentiments.[^r2]

### How does it work?
- Foci organize shared activities → valued interaction → positive sentiment and ties. Existing ties also encourage people to find or create new foci, which feed back into joint activities (Fig. 2).[^r3]
- The propositions connect shared foci, constraint, compatibility, and tie investment to transitivity, bridges, and ego-network density. These are qualitative tendencies; some ties arise by chance or adjacency without a focus, and other processes can override foci. No executable update schedule is specified.[^r3][^r4]

### What can it answer?
It supplies propositions for explaining and comparing transitivity, bridging, and personal-network density; the paper illustrates the theory with 1965–66 Detroit Area Study friendship patterns.[^r4]

### Where does it apply?
It applies to friendship networks where shared activities and extra-network contexts organize interaction; it is a theoretical framework, not a predictive parameterized model.[^r2]
