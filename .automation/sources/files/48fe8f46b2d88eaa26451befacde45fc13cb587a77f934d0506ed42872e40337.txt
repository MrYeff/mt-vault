## Abstract

Simulation has emerged as a popular method to study the long-term societal consequences of recommender systems. This approach allows researchers to specify their theoretical model explicitly and observe the evolution of system-level outcomes over time. However, performing simulation-based studies often requires researchers to build their own simulation environments from the ground up, which creates a high barrier to entry, introduces room for implementation error, and makes it difficult to disentangle whether observed outcomes are due to the model or the implementation. We introduce T-RECS, an open-sourced Python package designed for researchers to simulate recommendation systems and other types of sociotechnical systems in which an algorithm mediates the interactions between multiple stakeholders, such as users and content creators. To demonstrate the flexibility of T-RECS, we perform a replication of two prior simulation-based research on sociotechnical systems. We additionally show how T-RECS can be used to generate novel insights with minimal overhead. Our tool promotes reproducibility in this area of research, provides a unified language for simulating sociotechnical systems, and removes the friction of implementing simulations from scratch.

## Model

### What does it represent?
T-RECS is an event-driven framework for user–algorithm feedback systems with users, items, optional content creators, recommender state and metrics. It separates true user preferences/scores from the recommender's predicted preferences/scores.[^r3]

```yml
World:
  users: [User]
  items: [Item]
  recommender: Recommender
  creators: [Creator]?
  metrics: [Metric]

User:
  true_preferences: [f64]
  predicted_preferences: [f64]
  true_scores: [ItemId -> f64]
  predicted_scores: [ItemId -> f64]
  history: [Interaction]
```

### How does it work?
Each event step predicts user-item scores (default: preference/item-attribute inner product), presents ranked items with optional random interleaving, collects user feedback/consumption based on true preferences and updates system state from feedback. Metrics are then recorded. By default users provide one feedback per step in parallel; empty steps are skipped. Items may be fixed or generated by creators, whose probabilistic item distributions can adapt to feedback. Preference evolution, feedback behavior, ranking and metrics are configurable; no universal behavior law is imposed.[^r3]

```
sim_step(mut world: World):
  predictions = world.recommender.predict(world.users, world.items)
  slates = recommend(predictions, random_interleave=true)
  feedback = parallel_user_choice(slates, true_preferences=true)
  update_recommender(world, feedback)
  update_user_preferences_if_configured(world, feedback)
  update_creators_if_configured(world, feedback)
  record_metrics(world, feedback)
```

### What can it answer?
It supports controlled comparisons of sociotechnical simulation assumptions and outcomes; its reported demonstrations are conceptual replications plus a creator-feedback extension, not empirical validation of user behavior.[^r4]

### Where does it apply?
It applies to recommender-mediated multi-stakeholder systems and related user-item feedback processes. The loop above gives the framework defaults; each research model selects its own user, recommender, creator and metric implementations.[^r3]
